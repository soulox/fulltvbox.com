## The short version

- **The deal:** Fox Corporation agreed in June 2026 to acquire Roku for about **$22 billion** ($160/share, cash-and-stock), pairing Roku's streaming-device platform with Fox's live sports, news, and the Tubi service.
- **Timeline:** it's signed, not closed. Expect shareholder votes and antitrust review to run into the **first half of 2027** before anything actually changes hands.
- **For current owners:** nothing changes today. Your [Roku Streaming Stick 4K](/reviews/roku-streaming-stick-4k-2023) or [Roku Ultra](/reviews/roku-ultra-2024) works exactly as it did last month.
- **For buyers right now:** still a safe purchase — but go in knowing Fox content and advertising will likely get more prominent placement on the home screen once the deal closes.

If you use a Roku, or you're shopping for a streaming device and Roku is on your shortlist, you've probably seen the headline: a $22 billion deal that hands the country's most-used streaming platform to a broadcast and sports media company. Here's what's actually confirmed, what's still speculation, and what it should (and shouldn't) change about your buying decision.

## What was actually announced

In mid-June 2026, Fox Corporation and Roku announced a definitive agreement under which Fox will acquire Roku for **$160.00 per share**, paid in a mix of cash and Fox Class A common stock, valuing the deal at roughly **$22 billion** in enterprise value.

The pitch from both companies: Roku brings the largest connected-TV platform in the U.S. by hours streamed, a direct relationship with more than 100 million streaming households, and a growing ad business built on first-party viewing data. Fox brings live sports (NFL, MLB), FOX News Media, and the free ad-supported Tubi service. Combined, the companies say the deal positions them as the **third-largest player in U.S. television by share of viewing** — behind only the biggest legacy players.

Nothing about this is finalized. It's a signed agreement, not a completed sale. Roku shareholders still have to approve it, and a deal of this size will draw antitrust scrutiny given how central Roku has become to how Americans reach *every* streaming app, not just Fox's own. Both companies are targeting a close in the **first half of 2027** — meaning this is a slow-moving story, not an overnight switch.

## What changes for people who already own a Roku

Practically speaking: nothing, for a while. Roku's software, channel store, and app support don't change the day a deal like this is announced — they change (if at all) after it closes and the new owner starts integrating the platform, which by the companies' own timeline is at least half a year away.

Both companies have said publicly that Roku will keep operating as an **open, partner-friendly platform** — meaning your Netflix, YouTube, and other third-party apps aren't going anywhere, and Fox isn't locking Roku hardware to Fox-only content. That's the same arrangement Apple TV and Fire TV already operate under: one company can own the hardware and still be required to carry competitors' apps.

The more realistic changes to expect, once the deal actually closes, are softer ones: more promotion of Fox properties (Tubi, FOX Sports content, FOX News) on the Roku home screen, and Fox getting a bigger say in how advertising is sold across Roku's home screen and channel guide. Neither of those breaks your device or removes an app you rely on — but if you've used Roku specifically because it felt like a neutral, content-agnostic box, that neutrality is the thing most likely to erode over the next couple of years, not overnight.

## Should you still buy a Roku device right now

For most people, yes. If you're shopping today, the [Roku Streaming Stick 4K](/reviews/roku-streaming-stick-4k-2023) and [Roku Ultra](/reviews/roku-ultra-2024) will keep working and getting updates through at least 2027 under the current ownership and platform commitments — a pending acquisition doesn't shorten a device's useful life on its own.

**Where it's reasonable to hesitate:** if platform neutrality specifically matters to you — for instance, you want a box that has zero incentive to push one media company's content over another's — it's fair to weigh that against a Fire TV or Google TV device instead, since both come from companies with their own competing motivations but aren't in the middle of being acquired by a content publisher. Our [Fire TV vs Google TV vs Roku comparison](/guides/android-tv-vs-fire-tv-vs-roku) breaks down how the platforms differ today if you want to compare before deciding.

**Where it's not worth worrying:** don't delay a purchase you'd otherwise make just because of this deal. The timeline (into 2027, pending approval) is long enough that "wait and see" isn't really protecting you from anything — by the time the deal closes, review sites like this one will have a much clearer picture of what actually changed, and Roku hardware isn't getting bricked or degraded in the meantime.

## What's NOT worth worrying about yet

A dose of perspective, since deal announcements tend to generate more anxiety than the facts support:

- **"My Roku is going to stop supporting [app]."** Nothing in the announced deal removes third-party app support. Both companies have explicitly committed to keeping Roku open.
- **"Roku prices are about to jump."** No pricing changes have been announced for hardware or for Roku's ad-supported content. Speculation about future ad load or subscription bundling is just that — speculation — until the deal closes and integration actually begins.
- **"This is basically Apple TV now."** Not quite. Apple builds hardware and owns Apple TV+ content, but still ships every competitor's app. If regulators approve this deal, Fox owning Roku would follow a similar model — hardware/platform + content, with contractual and regulatory obligations to remain open — not a walled garden.

The real risk here isn't a sudden platform change, it's a slow drift in what gets front-page placement on the home screen. That's worth watching in 2027, not panicking about in 2026.
